I.6 · Reject an impossible inequality

If two angles of a triangle are equal to one another, the sides opposite those equal angles will also be equal to one another.

Hypothetical sketch · not all givens can hold

This scalene sketch shows the temporary supposition ΑΒ > ΑΓ. Its base angles are unequal. The proof retains equal base angles as a given and derives a contradiction: no Euclidean triangle satisfies both conditions.

Conditional comparison · these hypotheses cannot coexist

∠ΔΒΓ∠ΑΓΒequal in proof’s given, not in sketch

Given in proof: ∠ΑΒΓ = ∠ΑΓΒ · Suppose: ΑΒ > ΑΓ · Cut: ΔΒ = ΑΓ · I.4 would make part = whole → contradiction

The proposition

Ἐὰν τριγώνου αἱ δύο γωνίαι ἴσαι ἀλλήλαις ὦσιν, καὶ αἱ ὑπὸ τὰς ἴσας γωνίας ὑποτείνουσαι πλευραὶ ἴσαι ἀλλήλαις ἔσονται.

Si in triangulo duo anguli inter se aequales sunt, etiam latera sub aequalibus angulis subtendentia inter se aequalia erunt.

If two angles of a triangle are equal to one another, the sides opposite those equal angles will also be equal to one another.

1. Give the equal angles; claim the equal sides Given

Ἔστω τρίγωνον τὸ ΑΒΓ ἴσην ἔχον τὴν ὑπὸ ΑΒΓ γωνίαν τῇ ὑπὸ ΑΓΒ γωνίᾳ· λέγω, ὅτι καὶ πλευρὰ ἡ ΑΒ πλευρᾷ τῇ ΑΓ ἐστιν ἴση.

Sit triangulus ΑΒΓ habens ∠ ΑΒΓ = ΑΓΒ. dico, esse etiam ΑΒ = ΑΓ.

Let ΑΒΓ be a triangle with angle ΑΒΓ equal to ΑΓΒ. I say that side ΑΒ also equals ΑΓ.

The same nouns now exchange logical roles: γωνία (‘angle’) belongs to the given, πλευρά (‘side’) to the claim. This reverses the first conclusion of I.5; a converse requires its own proof.

Reason: The task and its given objects A given object is part of the problem. It has not been constructed by this proof. A finite straight line here means a line segment.

Printed source: Greek pp. 22; Latin pp. 23.

2. Suppose the sides are unequal Prove

εἰ γὰρ ἄνισός ἐστιν ἡ ΑΒ τῇ ΑΓ, ἡ ἑτέρα αὐτῶν μείζων ἐστίν. ἔστω μείζων ἡ ΑΒ,

Si enim ΑΒ rectae ΑΓ inaequalis est, alterutra earum maior est. sit ΑΒ maior,

For if ΑΒ is unequal to ΑΓ, one of them is greater. Let ΑΒ be the greater.

εἰ / si (‘if’) opens a temporary supposition. The prefix ἀν- in ἄνισος and in- in inaequalis negate equality. The new ἔστω does not replace the original equal-angle given.

Reason: Suppose the opposite · compare the lengths If ΑΒ and ΑΓ are unequal, one is greater. Call the greater one ΑΒ; the other case is obtained by exchanging Β and Γ. Euclid uses this ordering of lengths without listing it among his five postulates. The extra inequality is temporary, not another given of the theorem.

Printed source: Greek pp. 22; Latin pp. 23.

3. Cut ΔΒ equal to ΑΓ and join ΔΓ Construct

καὶ ἀφῃρήσθω ἀπὸ τῆς μείζονος τῆς ΑΒ τῇ ἐλάττονι τῇ ΑΓ ἴση ἡ ΔΒ, καὶ ἐπεζεύχθω ἡ ΔΓ.

et a maiore ΑΒ minori ΑΓ aequalis abscindatur ΔΒ, et ducatur ΔΓ.

From the greater ΑΒ cut off ΔΒ equal to the lesser ΑΓ, and join ΔΓ.

ΔΒ begins at Β: Δ lies inside ΑΒ. The source here uses ἐλάττονι, a variant of the ἐλάσσονι seen in I.3 and I.5. The cutting instruction has the same grammar and invokes the same earlier construction.

Reason: I.3 · cut off an equal part A shorter length can be cut off from a longer one. In I.5, extend ΑΕ far enough to exceed ΑΖ before cutting ΑΗ equal to ΑΖ. In I.6, the supposition ΑΒ > ΑΓ permits ΔΒ = ΑΓ, with Δ inside ΑΒ.

Printed source: Greek pp. 22; Latin pp. 23.

4. Apply I.4 under the supposition Prove

Ἐπεὶ οὖν ἴση ἐστὶν ἡ ΔΒ τῇ ΑΓ κοινὴ δὲ ἡ ΒΓ, δύο δὴ αἱ ΔΒ, ΒΓ δύο ταῖς ΑΓ, ΓΒ ἴσαι εἰσὶν ἑκατέρα ἑκατέρᾳ, καὶ γωνία ἡ ὑπὸ ΔΒΓ γωνίᾳ τῇ ὑπὸ ΑΓΒ ἐστιν ἴση·

iam cum ΔΒ = ΑΓ, et ΒΓ communis sit, duae rectae ΔΒ, ΒΓ duabus ΑΓ, ΓΒ aequales sunt altera alteri, et ∠ ΔΒΓ = ΑΓΒ.

Since ΔΒ equals ΑΓ and ΒΓ is common, the pairs ΔΒ, ΒΓ and ΑΓ, ΓΒ are respectively equal. Angle ΔΒΓ also equals ΑΓΒ by the given angle equality.

κοινὴ δὲ ἡ ΒΓ leaves ‘is’ understood: ‘and ΒΓ [is] common’. Latin writes communis sit. The angle equality is carried from the theorem’s given into the hypothetical comparison; it is not a measurement of the sketch.

Reason: I.4 applied inside the temporary supposition The cut supplies ΔΒ = ΑΓ; ΒΓ is common. Because Δ lies on ΒΑ, ∠ΔΒΓ is the given ∠ΑΒΓ, assumed equal to ∠ΑΓΒ. Thus I.4 would give equality of triangles ΔΒΓ and ΑΓΒ. The displayed scalene diagram cannot also satisfy the given angle equality.

Printed source: Greek pp. 24; Latin pp. 25.

5. A proper part would equal its whole Prove

βάσις ἄρα ἡ ΔΓ βάσει τῇ ΑΒ ἴση ἐστίν, καὶ τὸ ΔΒΓ τρίγωνον τῷ ΑΓΒ τριγώνῳ ἴσον ἔσται, τὸ ἔλασσον τῷ μείζονι· ὅπερ ἄτοπον·

itaque ΔΓ = ΑΒ et △ ΔΒΓ = ΑΓΒ, minus maiori; quod absurdum est.

Thus ΔΓ would equal ΑΒ, and triangle ΔΒΓ would equal ΑΓΒ: the lesser equal to the greater. That is absurd.

τὸ ἔλασσον τῷ μείζονι is compressed: ‘the lesser [equal] to the greater’. Latin minus maiori is equally brief. ἄτοπον (‘out of place; absurd’) signals the contradiction, not an approximate mismatch.

Reason: Common notion 5 · the whole is greater than the part Triangle ΔΒΓ is a proper part of ΑΓΒ, yet the supposition and I.4 have made them equal. Both claims cannot hold. Heiberg’s edition numbers the whole–part statement as common notion 8. The contradiction rejects the extra supposition of unequal sides.

Printed source: Greek pp. 24; Latin pp. 25.

6. Discharge the extra assumption Prove

οὐκ ἄρα ἄνισός ἐστιν ἡ ΑΒ τῇ ΑΓ· ἴση ἄρα.

itaque ΑΒ rectae ΑΓ inaequalis non est; aequalis igitur.

Therefore ΑΒ is not unequal to ΑΓ. It is therefore equal.

οὐκ … ἄνισος / inaequalis non est literally denies inequality. Then ἴση ἄρα / aequalis igitur gives the positive conclusion in just two words. The temporary supposition has been rejected.

Reason: Common notion 5 · the whole is greater than the part Triangle ΔΒΓ is a proper part of ΑΓΒ, yet the supposition and I.4 have made them equal. Both claims cannot hold. Heiberg’s edition numbers the whole–part statement as common notion 8. The contradiction rejects the extra supposition of unequal sides.

Printed source: Greek pp. 24; Latin pp. 25.

7. The converse is proved Conclude

Ἐὰν ἄρα τριγώνου αἱ δύο γωνίαι ἴσαι ἀλλήλαις ὦσιν, καὶ αἱ ὑπὸ τὰς ἴσας γωνίας ὑποτείνουσαι πλευραὶ ἴσαι ἀλλήλαις ἔσονται· ὅπερ ἔδει δεῖξαι.

Ergo si in triangulo duo anguli inter se aequales sunt, etiam latera sub aequalibus angulis subtendentia inter se aequalia erunt; quod erat demonstrandum.

Therefore, if two angles of a triangle are equal, the sides opposite those angles are also equal. Which was to be shown.

Ἐὰν … ὦσιν uses the subjunctive ‘if … are’ for a general condition, followed by future ἔσονται (‘will be’). Latin uses si … sunt followed by erunt. The heading’s conditional structure returns at the conclusion.

Reason: The theorem is proved The conclusion follows from the stated givens. ὅπερ ἔδει δεῖξαι and quod erat demonstrandum close a proof: ‘which was to be shown’. Compare ποιῆσαι, ‘to make’, at the end of the first three construction problems.

Printed source: Greek pp. 24; Latin pp. 25.

An impossible picture can still explain an argument

The middle steps use a scalene sketch to locate Δ and distinguish a part from its whole. Such a sketch cannot also have the two equal base angles required by the theorem. The plate labels that incompatibility and shows actual measured angles separately. The proof rejects the supposition of unequal sides; no impossible triangle has been constructed.

Definitions and starting permissions used in this proof

Common notion 5 in the usual list · ηʹ / VIII in Heiberg · Whole and proper part

Καὶ τὸ ὅλον τοῦ μέρους μεῖζόν [ἐστιν].

Et totum parte maius est.

And the whole [is] greater than the part.

Greek uses the genitive τοῦ μέρους after the comparative μεῖζον; Latin uses the ablative parte after maius. Both mean ‘greater than the part’. The brackets around [ἐστιν] belong to the printed Greek.

Printed source: Greek pp. 10; Latin pp. 11.

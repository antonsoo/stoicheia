## The six connected lessons

- I.11 raises a perpendicular from a point on a line. Obtain equal distances,
  use the equilateral construction and prove equal adjacent angles with I.8.
- I.12 drops a perpendicular from an exterior point. Choosing the auxiliary
  point on the other side guarantees a circle with two crossings. Moving that
  point changes the circle but preserves the constructed foot. An explicitly
  altered same-side choice can miss, touch, or cross the line; no fictitious
  pair of intersections or midpoint may be shown.
- I.13 proves that a linear pair equals two right angles by constructing a
  perpendicular and comparing sums. The two-right-angle case is distinguished
  from the unequal-angle argument.
- I.14 proves the converse. The sketch preserves the given angle pair and draws Ε off the line. It must be labeled hypothetical: it cannot also make ΓΒΕ a straight continuation.
- I.15 obtains vertical-angle equality by subtracting a shared angle from equal
  pairs. Retain the Greek bracketed corollary and the facing Latin omission.
- I.16 transports an angle into the exterior angle by a midpoint, equal extension
  and I.4. The second opposite angle needs a second construction, not an
  unearned appeal to the later triangle-angle-sum theorem.

## Review against the brief

The useful addition is a connected proof chain and its language, not six
independent animated pictures. In I.12 the auxiliary-point experiment exposes
why a short Greek phrase matters. In I.16 the construction exposes the work
needed before invoking any parallel postulate. Invalid and hypothetical figures
must keep those distinctions in SVG/HTML exports, shared state and offline use.
